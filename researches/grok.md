**There is no exact baked pair like “10.2° and 39.4 min” coming from Sūrya Siddhānta.**

### What the text actually gives

**Primary constant (10.1):**  
**12 bhāga** of angular separation.

That is the only fixed numerical threshold stated for the visibility limit.

**Operational method (10.2–10.5):**  
The text does **not** stop at a simple longitude test. It converts the actual Sun–Moon positional difference into a local rising/setting time difference (`lagnāntara-āsavaḥ` / `prāṇa`) and uses that interval. The lag is therefore a *derived* quantity, not a second independent constant.

### Classical equivalence (Babylonian → Indian → Islamic)

Across the early traditions the same rule appears in two equivalent forms:

| Form | Value | Source tradition |
|------|-------|------------------|
| Angular separation | **12°** | Sūrya Siddhānta, Pañcasiddhāntikā, Babylonian |
| Time lag (sunset → moonset) | **≈ 48 minutes** | Same traditions (12 equatorial degrees ≈ 48 min) |

The 48-minute figure is simply the rough time equivalent of 12° under average equatorial conditions. It is **not** an independent second threshold that must be AND-ed with a lower elongation.

### Why your 10° + 39 min works for 2023–29

It is a modern empirical tightening of the classical 12°/48 min rule.  
For the specific latitude, atmospheric conditions, and the particular set of new moons in those seven years, the combination

```
elongation ≥ 10°   AND   lag ≥ 39 min
```

happens to reproduce the observed/accepted first-visibility dates.  
That does **not** make 10 and 39 the “exact” Siddhāntic numbers. They are a tuned proxy that happens to fit your sample.

### What would be “exact” according to the Siddhānta

There is no closed-form pair of constants.  
The exact procedure is:

1. Compute true longitudes of Sun and Moon (with all usual corrections).
2. Apply the six-sign / same-sign logic of 10.2–10.3.
3. Convert the longitudinal difference into the corresponding *lagnāntara* in *prāṇa/asu*.
4. Iterate for the motions if necessary.
5. Decide visibility from the resulting time interval after sunset (or before sunrise).

Latitude of the observer, lunar latitude (`vikṣepa`), and the inclination of the ecliptic to the local horizon all enter through the rising-time calculation. That is why a fixed dual-threshold rule can never be a perfect substitute.

### Practical recommendation

- If you want **fidelity to Sūrya Siddhānta**: implement the *lagnāntara* procedure (or a modern spherical equivalent that uses the same geometric idea). The only baked constant is **12 bhāga**.
- If you want a simple modern filter that matches recent Indian visibility practice for your location: keep refining the empirical pair (your 10°/39 min is already good for 2023–29). There is no deeper “exact” number waiting inside the text.

The 12° of the Siddhānta remains the single classical constant; everything else is either a derived time interval or a modern empirical adjustment.
